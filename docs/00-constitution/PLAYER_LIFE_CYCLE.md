# Player life-cycle — levels 1 / 10 / 25 / 50 / 75 / 100

Status: ACCEPTED FOR GROUNDING — NUMBERS REMAIN TUNABLE

This document describes how the player's **life in the world** should change across the 1-100 journey.

It is not a stat table.

A level is meaningful when it changes some combination of:

- what the player can survive;
- what the player knows;
- where the player can reasonably go;
- how long an expedition can last;
- which people/places/services matter;
- what equipment carries history;
- how the class feels to inhabit;
- how much of the world feels familiar.

The intended first-character journey remains roughly 350-500 hours, but that duration is only valid if these stages contain different kinds of life rather than the same activity stretched across a larger XP curve.

---

# Level 1 — stranger at the edge of the world

## World relationship

The player knows almost nothing.

The first settlement is not "the tutorial hub." It is home because it is the only place the player understands.

The nearby road, farm, bridge, tree line and first dangerous landmark should already begin forming a mental map.

A place only a few minutes away can feel far because:

- the player does not know the route;
- recovery is weak;
- money is scarce;
- the toolkit is tiny;
- enemies are not yet familiar;
- dying far from town is disruptive.

## Typical life

The player:

- owns one small bag;
- carries poor/common equipment;
- has roughly two or three meaningful combat actions;
- has little money;
- has no travel network;
- has no mount;
- does not yet have a large profession identity;
- returns to the same few NPCs repeatedly;
- learns that trainer purchases and repairs compete with other spending.

A normal excursion may last only 20-40 minutes before bags, health/resources, uncertainty or danger create a reason to return.

That short radius is desirable.

The world should feel enormous because the player's life is still small.

## Combat

Routine single enemies are readable but not disposable.

An accidental second enemy can be frightening.

The player is learning:

- cast commitment;
- target choice;
- resource use;
- when to recover;
- which enemy animations/casts matter.

The class should not feel "unfinished," but it should feel young.

## Memory target

Within the first hours the player should already remember:

- the road that leads home;
- where they first nearly died;
- the first trainer purchase they could barely afford;
- one place they saw but chose not to enter;
- one item that felt meaningfully better than starting gear.

# Level 10 — local adventurer

## World relationship

The starting area is becoming familiar rather than obsolete.

The player knows several local paths without consulting the map.

The first settlement is still important, but the player has reasons to visit a larger nearby settlement or regional town.

Some locations first seen at level 1 remain dangerous.

The important emotional change is:

**the player now has a home region rather than only a home village.**

## Typical life

The player has:

- a recognizable early class toolkit, perhaps 5-7 active/contextual actions;
- several learned spell/ability ranks;
- an initial profession direction;
- better bags but still meaningful inventory pressure;
- a few pieces of equipment with stories attached;
- enough money to make choices, not enough to ignore prices;
- a small list of ongoing quest chains rather than only immediate errands.

A 1-2 hour expedition is now normal.

The player can leave with several intentions:

- continue a chain;
- gather;
- investigate a landmark;
- train/progress a profession;
- take a detour for an item/NPC;
- push farther down a known road.

They may return with some goals complete and others merely advanced.

## Travel

The player still walks almost everywhere.

The important progression is **knowledge**:

- which fork is shorter;
- which road is safer;
- where hostile camps are;
- where an inn sits;
- where a corpse run would be painful;
- which apparent shortcut is actually worse.

The map helps orient.

It does not replace learning.

## Combat

Routine fights are increasingly comfortable.

The player should be able to listen to something while fighting familiar enemies, but a dangerous caster, bad pull or rare enemy can still demand full attention.

Resources begin to create multi-fight decisions.

## Memory target

The player should have:

- one road they can travel from memory;
- one enemy family they understand;
- one memorable item still in active use;
- one unfinished long quest;
- one dangerous landmark they intend to revisit later.

---

# Level 25 — regional traveler

## World relationship

The player has outgrown being merely local.

They now understand that the world is composed of connected regions with different dangers, cultures, roads, materials and services.

The original province is **not finished**.

The player may return for:

- deeper subregions;
- an elite;
- a dungeon;
- profession materials/recipes;
- reputation;
- a class quest;
- a transport connection;
- a previously impossible ruin.

This is the first major proof that regions are not disposable level bands.

## Typical life

The player has:

- a real class identity;
- perhaps 8-12 regularly relevant actions plus situational tools;
- talent/specialization choices beginning to differentiate play;
- professions with enough investment that abandoning one would matter;
- a bank/storage relationship;
- multiple active quest chains spread across regions;
- some reputation history;
- enough equipment history that replacing a favorite item can be bittersweet rather than automatic.

Expeditions of 1-3 hours are normal.

The player may spend an entire evening:

- traveling toward a remote objective;
- completing only part of a chain;
- finding an unexpected cave;
- gathering along the way;
- returning late with full bags and low resources.

That is a successful session.

## Travel

The player has discovered useful infrastructure:

- inns;
- crossroads;
- ferries/carriages/other regional transport where appropriate;
- perhaps early flight-route connections if the world uses them at this stage.

Repeat journeys are shorter than first journeys because the player has learned and unlocked the world.

The continent must still feel large.

## Economy

Copper/silver/gold decisions have graduated from survival to planning.

Meaningful spending includes:

- training;
- profession investment;
- bags/storage;
- repair;
- transport;
- equipment opportunities.

The player should not yet feel economically independent of towns.

## Memory target

By level 25, the player should be able to tell stories tied to geography:

- "that was the road where...";
- "I used this weapon through...";
- "I avoided that cave for ten levels...";
- "I know an easier way through that forest...";
- "I always repair in that town before going north."

---

# Level 50 — established continental adventurer

## World relationship

The player now belongs to the continent in a meaningful way.

They have traveled far enough that several regions feel familiar, but there should still be major places that are unknown, remote or intimidating.

The world has not become a completed map.

Earlier areas are substantially safer, but not all their content is irrelevant.

Returning should make growth legible:

- common roadside threats may be trivial;
- old elite camps may now be manageable;
- some deep dungeon/rare/high-level layers may still matter;
- faction/profession/class reasons continue to create return trips.

## Typical life

The player has:

- a mature but not complete class toolkit;
- established talent identity without being trapped into a rigid modern spec model;
- several long-standing items/effects they can remember obtaining;
- significant profession skill and rare recipe aspirations;
- meaningful faction relationships;
- a network of preferred towns/inns/routes;
- several dungeons or remote destinations associated with past evenings of play.

A session can begin anywhere in a web of ongoing intentions rather than at a quest hub.

The player might log in and decide:

- continue a reputation arc;
- finish a profession objective;
- make a long trip for a class quest;
- revisit an old region;
- prepare for a dungeon;
- pursue a rare rumor;
- push into a new province.

## Travel

The player is highly knowledgeable but still geographically constrained.

They know transport routes and shortcuts.

They may deliberately choose a longer safe road or a dangerous cut.

The world has become **more navigable**, not smaller.

Mount acquisition should be approaching or still ahead depending on final pacing. If first mount remains around level 60, the level-50 player still understands the world primarily at walking scale.

## Combat

Routine enemies are familiar and efficient.

Difficult pulls, elites and dungeons ask for:

- interrupts;
- cooldowns;
- target priority;
- resource planning;
- class-specific utility.

The character has enough tools that difficult situations can be solved in different ways.

## Memory target

The character should now have a genuine personal history:

- towns associated with different eras of their journey;
- obsolete but memorable equipment;
- NPCs/factions they recognize;
- roads they no longer need a map to follow;
- places they once feared and can now handle;
- places still beyond them.

---

# Level 75 — veteran with a personal world

## World relationship

At this stage, progression is increasingly about **accumulated familiarity and access**, not merely higher numbers.

The player has a personal version of the world:

- preferred routes;
- known inns;
- favorite repair/trainer towns;
- shortcuts;
- transport links;
- remembered rare locations;
- factions they care about;
- regions they associate with old equipment and quest chains.

The continent is familiar enough to feel like a home, but remote lands and high-level threats still preserve discovery.

## Typical life

The player has:

- most of the recognizable class language;
- deep talent/ability interactions;
- established professions with difficult recipes/material goals;
- broad reputation history;
- a meaningful mount/travel network;
- enough wealth to plan large purchases without making money irrelevant;
- items that may have survived surprisingly long because their effects matter.

The player should be able to choose an evening's activity from several long-term threads.

No single "current zone" owns them.

## Travel

Mounts and transport have changed the cadence.

This is not permission to erase geography.

A trip that once took forty minutes may now take fifteen or twenty through learned infrastructure and mounted movement.

The player feels powerful partly because **they remember how long it used to take**.

Remote dungeons and expedition targets can still require significant first-time travel.

## Combat

The character is versatile.

Old ordinary enemies may be comfortably trivial.

Same-level and high-level enemies still require the established combat language rather than inflated reaction speed.

The player has enough utility that expertise shows through:

- interruption timing;
- efficient resource use;
- recovery avoidance;
- safe multi-pulls;
- control;
- positioning.

Routine play should still be allowed to breathe.

## Return

Earlier places still matter through selected systems:

- professions;
- reputations;
- long chains;
- class stories;
- dungeons;
- rare enemies;
- unique materials;
- social/economic services;
- world-state consequences.

Return should feel like revisiting one's own history, not artificial recycling.

---

# Level 100 — old character, not finished person

## World relationship

Level 100 is a culmination of the leveling journey, not the moment the "real game" begins.

The player has become someone who belongs almost everywhere they have traveled.

They possess:

- route knowledge;
- transport access;
- faction history;
- profession history;
- equipment history;
- quest consequences;
- class mastery;
- memories of danger that has since faded.

The strongest evidence of progression is not the character sheet.

It is the contrast between the player's current relationship with the continent and their level-1 relationship with it.

## Typical life

The player has:

- the complete broad class toolkit;
- mature talent choices;
- high profession investment;
- wide but not necessarily universal reputation;
- access to endgame-frontier threats and dungeons;
- a collection of remembered equipment and unique effects;
- established economic routines;
- a dense network of known locations and routes.

They can still have unfinished business.

Level cap should not imply:

- every reputation complete;
- every profession recipe known;
- every dungeon conquered;
- every rare found;
- every quest resolved;
- every region exhausted.

The world must remain larger than one progression bar.

## Power

Old low-level threats should now often be genuinely trivial.

That contrast is earned.

However, the path from level 1 to 100 should have been gradual enough that no recent part of the player's history was deleted every few levels.

Power should feel accumulated, not repeatedly replaced.

## Travel

The player's travel network is mature.

They can move efficiently because they spent hundreds of hours learning and connecting the world.

First-time travel to genuinely remote/new content can still be long.

The map is civilized through experience, not globally collapsed.

## Memory target

At level 100 the character should be capable of producing a long autobiography made from ordinary play:

- "I started there.";
- "that bridge used to scare me because...";
- "I carried this staff for twelve levels.";
- "I met that faction when...";
- "that road used to take forever.";
- "I remember when I couldn't enter those ruins.";
- "we spent an entire night reaching and clearing that dungeon.";
- "I still use that inn when I'm in this part of the world."

If level 100 produces only "my numbers are bigger," the progression model failed.

# What changes numerically versus experientially

## Numerical growth should provide

- gradual survivability increase;
- gradual damage/healing/resource improvement;
- broader equipment/stat budgets;
- real advantage over old threats;
- access to increasingly demanding content.

## Experiential growth should provide

- route knowledge;
- transport infrastructure;
- familiarity with enemy families;
- profession investment;
- reputation relationships;
- remembered equipment;
- long-running quest consequences;
- more confident expedition planning;
- reasons to return to old places;
- a larger personal radius of safety and competence.

The numerical layer supports the experiential layer.

It does not replace it.

---

# Cross-stage invariants

At every representative level:

1. Towns must still have a reason to exist.
2. The player must have at least one unresolved future destination.
3. Some equipment should be old enough to remember.
4. The quest log may contain objectives older than the current session.
5. Recovery/travel/inventory decisions must remain possible without becoming chores.
6. The player should know some routes and still be learning others.
7. Current content must coexist with remembered older content.
8. Routine combat may be calm.
9. Dangerous situations must contrast with routine play.
10. Progression must add history, not simply replace it.

# Grounding tests derived from this document

Future design/playtest work should be able to answer:

- At level 10, can the player navigate at least one meaningful route without guidance?
- At level 25, does the starting province contain a reason to return that is not pure nostalgia?
- At level 50, can a player describe several concurrent goals in different systems/regions?
- At level 75, have mounts/transport made known travel easier without making geography irrelevant?
- At level 100, are major non-level progression threads still capable of being unfinished?
- Across all stages, can the player name equipment, places and journeys they remember from earlier stages?

If those answers become "no," increasing XP requirements or content volume is not an acceptable fix.
