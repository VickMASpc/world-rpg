# Progression doctrine

Status: ACCEPTED FOR GROUNDING — ORIGINAL LONG-FORM TARGETS PRESERVED

World RPG is built around **100 levels** and a first-character journey measured in hundreds of hours.

The current accepted planning target remains roughly **350-500 hours** for a blind first character.

This is not a target to be achieved by inflating XP requirements.

It is only valid if the world contains enough meaningful life to occupy that time.

The original accepted cumulative shape remains a working target:

- level 1-10: roughly 8-12 hours total;
- level 10-25: roughly 35-50 hours total;
- level 25-50: roughly 100-140 hours total;
- level 50-75: roughly 200-280 hours total;
- level 75-100: roughly 350-500 hours total.

These are pacing bands, not promises that every player or class lands on the same minute.

---

# Leveling is the game

Level 100 is not when World RPG begins.

The 1-100 journey is the central game.

Therefore leveling must carry:

- class growth;
- gear history;
- trainer decisions;
- profession progression;
- reputation;
- travel-network growth;
- world familiarity;
- wealth;
- access to new places and quests;
- gradually changing relationships with old places.

XP is only one progression axis.

A level that changes nothing except a number is acceptable occasionally.

A progression model where almost every level changes nothing except a number is not.

---

# Slow levels

Later levels may take several hours.

That is intended.

The player may spend multiple real sessions at the same level.

This allows:

- equipment to remain relevant;
- quest chains to span levels;
- regions to remain active;
- reputation/profession goals to coexist with leveling;
- class abilities to have time to become familiar before more are added.

The game must resist the instinct to constantly reward the player with a new button or item simply because a level took a long time.

Time itself is not a problem.

Meaningless repetition is.

---

# Class growth

Classes should grow gradually.

The original idea includes:

- many casts;
- meaningful cast times/channels;
- spell/ability ranks;
- trainers;
- a large but learned toolkit.

A new ability should have time to become part of the player's habits before another layer is added.

The toolkit should not be front-loaded at low level merely because modern games expect immediate completeness.

At the same time, early classes must already feel like themselves.

Level 1 is inexperienced, not featureless.

---

# Ability ranks and trainers

Trainer-based growth is part of the heavy-RPG identity.

Ranks can provide:

- stronger versions of existing spells;
- improved resource efficiency;
- longer duration;
- stronger secondary effects;
- access to new utility.

Not every rank needs a dramatic transformation.

The trainer relationship matters because:

- town visits matter;
- money matters;
- class growth has a physical place;
- the player remembers becoming stronger.

Training costs should compete with other spending without becoming a punitive tax.

---

# Multiple progression tracks

Important progression includes:

- character level;
- class toolkit;
- ability ranks;
- talents;
- equipment;
- professions;
- reputation;
- travel discovery;
- transport network;
- wealth;
- quest access;
- world knowledge.

These tracks should move at different speeds.

A character may gain:

- a level without replacing gear;
- a useful recipe without gaining XP;
- a reputation rank without learning an ability;
- a shortcut that permanently improves travel;
- enough money for a trainer purchase after several sessions.

That unevenness makes the character's history less mechanical.

---

# Gear progression

Gear must age slowly.

The accepted direction is that a good item may remain relevant for roughly **10-20 hours**, sometimes longer.

An item may survive many levels.

This is not a guaranteed replacement schedule.

It is a protection against disposable loot.

A piece can remain useful because:

- its raw stats are still competitive;
- its unique effect suits the character;
- alternatives have not appeared;
- the player values a particular tradeoff.

The player should remember equipment.

Replacing a favorite weapon after many hours can be a meaningful moment.

---

# Compressed power growth

Vertical scaling must remain restrained.

The project rejects a model where:

- ten levels multiply damage/health many times over;
- every old item becomes garbage immediately;
- every old enemy becomes irrelevant after a few levels;
- every region is a disposable bracket.

Numerical growth must still be real.

The player should eventually overpower old threats.

But that should happen gradually over **dozens of levels**, not instantly.

---

# Relationship to lower-level enemies

These are qualitative working expectations derived from the original doctrine, not frozen equations.

## About +5 levels above an enemy

The player should feel stronger.

The fight should be easier and safer.

The enemy should still be a real combatant, not decorative clutter.

## About +10 levels

The advantage should be obvious.

Routine enemies may require much less attention and fewer resources.

They should not automatically evaporate from one incidental hit.

## About +20 levels

Most ordinary enemies from that older band should be easy or effectively trivial.

Special enemies, deep pockets, elites, dungeons or later-layer content in the same geography may still matter.

## About +30 levels and beyond

Ordinary low-level enemies may be genuinely trivial.

That is earned progression.

The region can still remain relevant through:

- professions;
- reputation;
- quest chains;
- class content;
- dungeons;
- rare enemies;
- transport;
- economy;
- higher-level subregions.

The world remains relevant even when some inhabitants no longer threaten the player.

---

# No global level scaling

The world must not silently scale every enemy to the player.

A dangerous region should be dangerous because it is dangerous.

A low-level farm should eventually feel safe because the character grew.

This contrast is a core progression reward.

Selective authored scaling for specific exceptional systems may exist only if the design explicitly needs it.

It must never become the default solution.

---

# Professions

Professions are long-form progression.

They should deepen over weeks, not resolve into one afternoon of checklist crafting.

Progress may involve:

- gathering skill;
- recipe discovery;
- trainer ranks;
- rare materials;
- reputation;
- geographic knowledge;
- dungeon/elite ingredients;
- economic specialization.

A profession should create reasons to revisit places at different character levels.

Profession progress is part of why the world remains layered.

---

# Reputation

Reputation should represent a relationship accumulated through time.

It can unlock:

- quests;
- recipes;
- services;
- transport;
- equipment;
- social/world recognition.

It should not exist merely as another bar to fill.

Reputation is valuable when the player associates it with actual people, towns and regions.

---

# Wealth progression

Money should remain meaningful for a long time.

Early wealth pressure is strong.

The player must choose among:

- trainers;
- repairs;
- food/drink;
- reagents;
- bags/storage;
- profession investment;
- transport;
- equipment opportunities.

Later wealth increases, but money should not become completely irrelevant.

Higher-level spending can include:

- mounts;
- advanced training;
- profession investments;
- expensive repairs;
- rare purchases;
- travel/service costs.

The exact economy requires simulation and content budgets later.

The doctrine is simply that wealth is part of progression, not a cosmetic counter.

---

# Mount progression

The working original direction keeps the first mount late, around **level 60**.

By then the player should have spent a very large amount of time experiencing the continent at walking scale.

The first mount is a progression milestone because it changes the player's relationship with known roads.

It is not a correction for allegedly bad early travel.

---

# Death and progression

The game is already slow.

Death can cost:

- time;
- corpse recovery;
- position;
- repair;
- expedition momentum.

It should not casually erase long-term progression through:

- routine XP loss;
- permanent gear destruction;
- loss of hours of character advancement.

Difficulty should create tension without treating the player's invested time as disposable.

---

# Progression anti-patterns

Reject:

- XP inflation used to hide insufficient content;
- global level scaling;
- gear replacement every few levels by design;
- exponential health/damage growth;
- class kits front-loaded so early leveling has nowhere to grow;
- every level requiring a flashy reward;
- profession progression disconnected from geography;
- wealth becoming irrelevant early;
- old regions being deleted by the level curve.

---

# Progression validation

Future P4 work should validate doctrine-derived relationships, especially:

- levels 1 / 10 / 25 / 50 / 75 / 100;
- +5 / +10 / +20 / +30 level advantages;
- same item across several levels;
- same region revisited after many levels;
- trainer/economy pressure over representative stages;
- class toolkit growth without button flooding.

The simulator exists to test these relationships.

The relationships do not exist because the simulator can test them.
