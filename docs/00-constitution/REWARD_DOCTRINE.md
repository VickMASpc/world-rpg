# Reward doctrine

Status: ACCEPTED FOR GROUNDING — ORIGINAL SCARCITY/MEMORY PRINCIPLES PRESERVED

Rewards in World RPG should accumulate into memory.

The game rejects the idea that every few minutes must produce:

- a new item;
- a stronger number;
- a chest;
- a rarity flash;
- a completed bar.

The original reward fantasy is slower:

- a useful item lasts;
- a rare creature is actually rare;
- a long quest may pay off much later;
- money has uses;
- a profession recipe changes future plans;
- reaching an inn or transport node can itself be progress.

---

# Equipment rewards

Equipment should be scarce enough that upgrades matter.

A good item may remain equipped for **10-20 hours or more**.

The player may gain several levels without replacing a slot.

The game should avoid:

- constant green/blue item rain;
- automatic replacement by level number;
- tiny meaningless stat upgrades every session.

A new item is valuable when the player can answer:

- where it came from;
- why it was better;
- what they replaced;
- how long they used the old one.

---

# Item identity

Items may matter for more than raw power.

Identity can come from:

- unusual stat mix;
- proc/effect;
- class synergy;
- profession relevance;
- resistance;
- resource behavior;
- utility;
- appearance/lore where appropriate.

Compressed scaling allows an older item with a good effect to remain useful.

This is desirable.

The system should not force replacement simply because a newer item has a higher hidden tier.

---

# Quest rewards

Not every quest needs a gear reward.

Valid rewards include:

- money;
- reputation;
- trainer/class access;
- profession recipes/materials;
- information;
- world access;
- transport;
- an item;
- a follow-up chain;
- relationship changes;
- discovery of a new place.

A long quest may be worth doing because of the journey and consequence, not because a UI chest appears at the end.

The strongest quest rewards can be remembered for many levels.

---

# Dungeon rewards

Dungeon rewards should be important enough that the player remembers the dungeon.

This does not require every run to replace gear.

A dungeon may offer:

- a specific sought-after item;
- profession material;
- quest objective;
- reputation;
- recipe;
- rare drop;
- access to another chain.

If a player replaces half their equipment every dungeon evening, gear history disappears.

---

# Rare rewards

Rare means rare.

A rare enemy should not feel like a routine scheduled loot node.

The player may:

- hear about it first;
- see evidence;
- encounter it unexpectedly;
- fail to kill it;
- return later.

The reward may be:

- unique item;
- recipe;
- reputation;
- quest trigger;
- material;
- simply the encounter itself.

Recognition and memory are part of the reward.

---

# Profession rewards

Profession progression should produce meaningful outcomes:

- useful equipment;
- consumables;
- reagents;
- recipes;
- economic value;
- access to specialized materials.

Profession rewards should connect to geography.

A recipe or material can create a reason to return to an old region long after its routine enemies became easy.

---

# Travel rewards

Travel progression is itself rewarding.

Examples:

- discovering an inn;
- finding a shortcut;
- reaching a ferry;
- learning a carriage route;
- discovering a flight/transport point;
- eventually earning a mount.

These rewards improve the player's relationship with geography.

They are not merely convenience unlocks.

---

# Reputation rewards

Reputation rewards should feel like growing acceptance or access.

Possible outcomes:

- new quests;
- services;
- recipes;
- equipment;
- transport;
- access to restricted places;
- better prices where appropriate.

The reward should remain tied to the faction/place rather than functioning as a detached global currency ladder.

---

# Money as reward

Money matters because there are meaningful sinks.

Receiving money can be satisfying when the player is deciding between:

- trainer ranks;
- repairs;
- bags;
- profession investment;
- consumables/reagents;
- transport;
- saving for a mount;
- a rare vendor purchase.

Gold loses meaning if every important purchase is automatic or if income greatly outpaces all costs.

The game should not create artificial money sinks solely to delete currency.

---

# Level-up rewards

A level itself is a reward.

It may provide:

- stat growth;
- access to training;
- new quest availability;
- talent progression;
- equipment eligibility;
- increased confidence against older enemies.

Not every level needs:

- a new ability;
- a new item;
- a dramatic animation.

The journey is long enough that subtle levels are acceptable.

---

# Discovery as reward

Finding something can be enough.

Examples:

- a hidden path;
- a dangerous ruin;
- a high-level area seen early;
- a trainer;
- an herbalist;
- a rare spawn location;
- a cave entrance;
- a future dungeon.

Some discoveries pay off immediately.

Some become intentions carried for many levels.

That delayed payoff is part of the game's memory structure.

---

# Reward frequency

World RPG deliberately avoids constant reward cadence.

There may be long stretches where the player receives:

- XP;
- materials;
- money;
- quest progress;
- knowledge

without receiving a major new item.

This is acceptable.

The game must not manufacture reward spam to compensate for its slow pace.

The slow pace is intentional.

---

# Reward anti-patterns

Reject:

- loot showers;
- mandatory item upgrade every session;
- rarity inflation;
- quest rewards chosen only by item level;
- every rare having a trivial respawn/farm loop;
- every dungeon run showering replacements;
- profession rewards detached from world geography;
- constant celebratory UI for ordinary progression;
- money sinks with no world logic;
- rewards so frequent that individual items/quests become forgettable.

---

# Reward validation

A healthy character should be able to remember:

- a weapon used for many hours;
- where it came from;
- one long quest whose reward mattered;
- a profession recipe that changed behavior;
- a transport/shortcut discovery;
- a rare encounter;
- an expensive purchase that required saving.

If the inventory contains dozens of recent interchangeable upgrades and none have stories, reward pacing failed.
