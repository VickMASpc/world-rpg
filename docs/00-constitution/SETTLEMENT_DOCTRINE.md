# Settlement doctrine

Status: ACCEPTED FOR GROUNDING — SERVICES/DENSITY REMAIN CONTENT-SPECIFIC

Settlements are expedition anchors.

They exist so that leaving civilization and returning to civilization have meaning.

A settlement may be:

- village;
- town;
- city;
- fort;
- inn/crossroads;
- profession camp;
- monastery;
- remote outpost.

They do not all need the same services.

Their differences are part of geography.

---

# Core purpose

Settlements provide some combination of:

- safety;
- orientation;
- rest;
- repair;
- vendors;
- class trainers;
- profession trainers/work areas;
- bank/storage;
- inn/home binding;
- transport;
- quest/story contacts;
- rumors/information;
- social/world texture.

The player should develop preferences:

- "I repair there before heading north."
- "That town has my trainer."
- "I store profession materials in the city."
- "That inn is the last safe place before the pass."

That is settlement identity.

---

# Town as punctuation

A town visit is not a lobby between combat instances.

It is punctuation in an expedition rhythm.

Returning may mean:

- bags are full;
- equipment is damaged;
- resources are low;
- quests are ready to report;
- training is available;
- profession work accumulated;
- new route/transport unlocked;
- money can finally be spent.

A long expedition makes ordinary services satisfying.

---

# Service distribution

Do not put every possible service in every settlement.

A small village may have:

- inn;
- general goods;
- simple repair;
- a few local contacts.

A regional town may add:

- trainers;
- professions;
- storage;
- transport.

A major city may provide a broad service network.

Exact service maps belong to world/content design.

The doctrine is that settlement hierarchy should be legible through services.

This creates reasons to travel between places even when enemies are no longer challenging.

---

# Trainers

Class and profession trainers belong physically in the world.

Training provides:

- a reason to return;
- class identity;
- economic pressure;
- memorable progression moments.

Trainer availability need not be universal.

A remote settlement may not support every class/profession.

This is acceptable when the travel requirement remains consistent with the broader world design.

---

# Repair

Repair is part of expedition preparation/return.

It should:

- matter over extended activity;
- cost money;
- encourage maintenance before remote travel.

It should not:

- cause equipment to break constantly;
- interrupt ordinary play every few minutes;
- permanently destroy long-lived gear through routine use.

Exact durability/repair curves remain later tuning work.

---

# Storage

Storage matters because inventory is finite and professions/long quests accumulate objects.

Storage should help the player maintain a long-lived character history.

It should not become unlimited field inventory.

Bank/storage access primarily belongs to civilization or specifically justified world infrastructure.

The player may need to decide what to carry on an expedition.

That is desirable.

---

# Inns

Inns are especially important.

They can provide:

- safe stopping point;
- home/hearth binding;
- food/drink;
- rumors;
- local contacts;
- geographic identity;
- transition between expeditions.

The player should remember inns because they repeatedly stage journeys from them.

An inn can be meaningful even when it contains no formal quest.

---

# Professions in settlements

Professions connect towns to the wider world.

Settlements may contain:

- trainers;
- crafting stations;
- specialist vendors;
- recipe sources;
- buyers/sellers;
- profession-related quests.

Different places may become important to different professions.

This creates return behavior that survives level progression.

---

# Transport

Transport nodes are settlement services, not a global menu.

Examples:

- ferry dock;
- carriage stop;
- ship;
- discovered flight/transport connection.

A settlement becomes more valuable when it joins the player's travel network.

The player generally must reach/discover the place before benefiting from the route.

---

# Rumors and information

Settlements can provide information without immediately converting it into quest markers.

Examples:

- an NPC mentions dangerous ruins;
- a guard warns about a road;
- an innkeeper mentions a rare creature;
- a profession NPC discusses a material source;
- travelers describe another settlement.

Information can create future intentions.

Not every rumor requires a formal tracked quest.

---

# Settlement population

Not every NPC exists to sell, train, or give a quest.

Settlements need enough ordinary life that useful NPCs feel like inhabitants rather than interface buttons.

This does not require heavy simulation of every citizen.

The goal is credibility and place identity.

---

# Safety

Settlement safety is relative.

A village may be threatened.

An outpost may sit beside dangerous territory.

A city may contain story conflict.

But settlements normally provide a lower-attention state than wilderness.

The player should feel the contrast between arriving somewhere supported and remaining remote.

---

# Field convenience boundary

Field convenience becomes too strong when it removes the reason to return to or care about settlements.

Be conservative with portable access to:

- repair;
- bank/storage;
- trainers;
- profession infrastructure;
- auction/market systems if any;
- transport;
- full resupply.

Some field tools may exist.

Examples:

- class recovery;
- limited camp;
- portable consumables;
- profession-specific tools.

But field convenience should extend an expedition, not transform wilderness into a town UI.

The test is:

**Does this convenience remove a meaningful settlement relationship?**

If yes, it requires strong justification.

---

# Remote refuges

Remote inns/outposts can extend expedition reach.

They are especially valuable because the world is large.

A small refuge does not need every service.

Providing only:

- safe logout;
- food/drink;
- limited repair;
- a few local contacts

may already change the player's relationship with a remote region.

This is good travel progression.

---

# Major cities

A major city should feel qualitatively different from a village.

Possible differences:

- service density;
- trainer breadth;
- profession districts;
- storage/economy;
- transport connections;
- reputation/faction presence;
- many social/story threads.

A city should not merely be a larger cluster of quest markers.

It should function as infrastructure for a long-lived character.

---

# Returning to old settlements

Old settlements should remain useful beyond their original enemy level.

Reasons include:

- transport;
- profession specialists;
- reputation;
- class trainer/quest;
- unique vendor;
- dungeon access;
- bank/storage preference;
- geographic position;
- ongoing story.

The player may eventually visit less often.

The doctrine does not require every village to remain equally important forever.

It requires the world not to systematically erase settlements as soon as their local mobs are outleveled.

---

# Settlement anti-patterns

Reject:

- every settlement containing every service;
- every NPC being a quest/vendor interface;
- universal field access to town services;
- cities that function only as menus;
- villages that become completely useless once local quests are complete;
- identical service layouts copied everywhere;
- mandatory town chores with no decision;
- teleport hubs that erase geographic relationships.

---

# Settlement playtest questions

When first reaching a settlement:

- Can the player explain why reaching it matters besides quest completion?
- Does it change expedition reach or available services?

After many hours:

- Does the player have preferred settlements for different purposes?
- Can they remember where important trainers/services are?

For a remote outpost:

- Does it make the surrounding wilderness feel more reachable without becoming a full city?

For field convenience:

- Does the feature extend an expedition, or does it make civilization unnecessary?

If towns are merely safe menus between objectives, settlement design failed.
