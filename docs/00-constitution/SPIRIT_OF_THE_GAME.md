# Spirit of the game

Status: ACCEPTED AS NORTH STAR

This document exists to stop World RPG from becoming an impressive framework for a game we no longer remember how to make.

The project is not primarily about registries, codecs, casts, JSON, Fabric, formulas, or the number of systems we can build.

It is about creating a world that a player can **live in for months**.

## The sentence

**Slow should mean spacious, gradual, and lived-in — not padded.**

Everything else follows from that.

World RPG should feel like a huge, slow-burn RPG world that happens to use Minecraft as its engine.

Minecraft is not the fantasy being sold. The world, character, journey, memory, distance, risk, and accumulation of familiarity are.

## What the player should eventually say

Not:

- "I cleared that zone."
- "I finished the quest hub."
- "I hit the next gear tier."
- "I unlocked fast travel."

But:

- "I know this road."
- "The forest is safer if you stay east of the river."
- "I remember when those ruins were way above my level."
- "I got this staff three nights ago and I still use it."
- "I have been carrying this quest for days and I'm finally near the cave."
- "I'm low on mana and my bags are full. I'm going home."
- "Thank God, an inn."
- "I finally made it to the northern city."
- "I remember seeing this place twenty levels ago."

The game succeeds when geography, items, quests and routines become memories.

## The primary fantasy

The fantasy is not constant heroism.

It is becoming a person who gradually belongs to an enormous world.

At first:

- the world is unknown,
- roads are confusing,
- distant enemies are terrifying,
- money is scarce,
- the player's toolkit is small,
- travel is slow,
- towns are important.

Hundreds of hours later:

- the player recognizes roads without a map,
- has personal shortcuts,
- remembers dangerous camps,
- understands regional enemy families,
- owns equipment with history,
- has relationships with factions and towns,
- has built a transport network through discovery,
- carries a large but familiar class toolkit,
- can return to places that once frightened them and feel genuine growth.

Progression is therefore partly numerical and partly **familiarity**.

## Leveling is the game

The 1-100 journey is not an extended tutorial before endgame.

It is the central work.

A current planning target is roughly 350-500 hours for a blind first character, but the number is not the goal. The goal is that the player can spend those hours:

- travelling,
- fighting,
- exploring,
- getting lost,
- preparing,
- recovering,
- learning routes,
- following long quests,
- working professions,
- finding equipment,
- returning to places,
- discovering transport,
- building reputation,
- slowly mastering a class.

If hundreds of hours require repetitive filler, the design has failed.

## Geography is progression

The continent must feel larger at level 5 than it does at level 70, even if the physical terrain has not changed.

Early movement is mostly physical.

The player learns:

- roads,
- crossings,
- bridges,
- ferries,
- paths,
- inns,
- graveyards,
- shortcuts,
- danger zones.

Later, travel becomes easier because the player has **civilized their personal map** through discovery.

The world must not collapse into a menu of destinations.

## Places are not disposable

A region is not a level bracket that becomes dead content.

One place may contain:

- safe farms for low levels,
- deeper wilderness for mid levels,
- ruins that are initially too dangerous,
- a dungeon discovered much later,
- class/profession/reputation reasons to return at high level.

A player should be able to know a place for dozens of levels.

The return is important.

## Travel has rhythm

An hour-long journey is only valid if it is a journey.

It must not be an hour of holding forward.

Travel may contain:

- familiar quiet road,
- changing terrain,
- landmarks,
- forks,
- dangerous shortcuts,
- occasional enemies,
- weather,
- settlements,
- travelers,
- hidden places,
- moments of getting lost,
- long stretches where absolutely nothing demands attention.

The game is allowed to be quiet.

Five minutes of road, ambience and distant scenery is not a content failure.

Constant stimulation would make the world feel like an amusement park.

## Roads matter

Roads are an actual world system.

They should generally be:

- safer,
- clearer,
- somewhat faster,
- connected to civilization.

They are not always shortest.

The player can choose between certainty and risk.

Eventually the player develops personal routes that are not explicitly awarded by a UI.

## Towns are punctuation

Towns and inns need emotional weight because expeditions have weight.

A return to town should involve ordinary, grounding actions:

- sell,
- repair,
- train,
- organize,
- cook/eat,
- buy supplies,
- talk,
- check professions,
- bind hearth,
- log off.

After a long expedition, the warmth and safety of an inn should feel different from the wilderness.

This ordinary rhythm is part of the fantasy.

## The expedition is a core unit of play

The basic quest loop must not be:

accept -> walk 90 seconds -> kill 8 -> return.

A major play rhythm is:

town preparation
-> choose a direction
-> leave civilization
-> spend one to three hours moving through the world
-> complete some objectives
-> partially advance others
-> gather
-> discover
-> fight
-> maybe die
-> fill bags
-> decide to return
-> make the journey home.

The words **"I'm going home"** should mean something.

## Combat breathes

The player does not need to be on fire all the time.

Routine enemies should often be comfortable.

Stronger enemies, packs, elites and bosses progressively demand more attention.

The same character should support:

- calm leveling while listening to something,
- sudden moments that force full attention,
- long resource decisions across several fights,
- deliberate casts and channels,
- interrupts and tactical reactions.

Not every boar is a raid boss.

Not every fight resets the player perfectly to 100%.

## Casting has weight

World RPG should not be afraid of commitment.

Normal casts may take roughly 1.5-3 seconds.

Some abilities can take 4 seconds.

Some rituals or special actions may take 5-6 seconds.

Channels may last several seconds.

Standing still to finish something powerful should sometimes be the point.

The game is not trying to imitate the constant-motion reflex of modern action MMOs.

## Attrition makes ordinary fights matter

Mana, health, consumables and recovery should matter across a sequence.

A mage may finish:

- fight one at 82% mana,
- fight two at 61%,
- fight three at 37%.

The next decision is not automatically "pull again."

It may be:

- drink,
- continue,
- use a potion,
- change target,
- take a safer route,
- return to camp.

Small decisions make slow play textured.

## Quests are adventures, not errands

A quest may take ninety minutes.

A chain may stay in the journal across several real days.

The journal records things the character is involved in; it is not a conveyor belt of ten-minute chores.

The player should sometimes realize:

"I've had this for three days and I'm finally here."

That is desirable.

## Items become memories

Equipment should not be replaced every session.

A good item may remain relevant for ten or twenty hours, occasionally longer.

A weapon can matter because:

- it was hard to obtain,
- its effect suits the build,
- the player remembers the dungeon,
- it lasted across many levels.

Compressed vertical scaling is required so old items and old places age gracefully.

## Power is real but not explosive

Progress must eventually make old dangers easy.

But ten levels should not turn every older enemy into visual clutter that dies instantly.

The player should outgrow places over dozens of levels, not a handful.

The world should age behind the character instead of being deleted behind them.

## Rare means rare

A rare creature is not a yellow outline on a sixty-second respawn.

The player may hear about something long before seeing it.

When it finally appears unexpectedly, the recognition is the reward.

The world should retain some mystery even after many hours.

## Time of day and weather create texture

The game does not need hardcore survival.

It does need atmosphere with consequences.

Examples:

- fog hurts navigation,
- night changes the emotional read of a forest,
- some NPCs stop working,
- a ferry may stop at night,
- different creatures appear,
- an inn becomes more attractive because travelling further feels stupid.

The purpose is not punishment.

The purpose is that journeys differ.

## Failure has weight, not cruelty

The game is already slow.

Death can mean:

- corpse run,
- recovery,
- lost position,
- repair cost,
- disrupted expedition.

It should not casually delete an hour of progress through XP loss or permanent equipment destruction.

Slow is not permission to disrespect the player's time.

## Things World RPG must resist

- Disposable theme-park zones.
- Global level scaling.
- GPS arrows replacing navigation.
- Unrestricted map teleportation.
- Constant combat density.
- Constant dopamine events.
- Loot showers.
- Gear replacement every few minutes.
- Quest hubs made of ten-minute chores.
- Empty huge terrain mistaken for immersion.
- Survival busywork that only interrupts play.
- Punishment whose only purpose is extending playtime.
- Architecture that exists because it is elegant rather than because the player experience needs it.
- Building content volume before knowing what that content should feel like.

## Final test

The first vertical slice is not successful because it contains a town, dungeon, thirty quests, and fifteen enemy types.

It is successful if after roughly fifteen hours the player has a mental map.

They should remember:

- which bridge leads home,
- which road has bandits,
- where an herbalist lives,
- where a memorable item came from,
- an enemy they still cannot beat,
- a useful shortcut,
- a ruin they intend to revisit.

If that happens, we have the RPG.

If we need floating arrows, constant combat, loot explosions and stimulation every thirty seconds to keep the player interested, we do not.
